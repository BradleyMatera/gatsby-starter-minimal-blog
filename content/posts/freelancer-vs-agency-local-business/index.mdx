A freelancer is the right choice for a local business that needs a 5 to 15 page website, wants direct communication with the person building it, and has a budget under $2,000. An agency is the right choice when you need a large custom build, ongoing marketing services, or a team to handle multiple workstreams. Neither is universally better. The decision depends on scope, budget, and how much process you need around the project.

I am a freelancer. I am going to be honest about when that is the wrong choice, because sending you to an agency when you need one is better for both of us. This post compares the two fairly so you can make the right call.

## Where they are the same

Before comparing, here is what a good freelancer and a good agency have in common:

- A portfolio of real work you can review
- Clear pricing and a written scope of work
- A contract that specifies what you own when the project is done
- Responsive communication during the project
- A site that is mobile responsive, fast, and SEO-ready

If either one cannot show you these things, do not hire them. The freelancer vs agency question only matters after you have confirmed the basics.

## When an agency is the right choice

### Large scope projects

If you need a 30-page site with custom integrations, a booking system, e-commerce, a customer portal, and multi-language support, an agency is the right call. A solo developer can build a 15-page site, but a project that size with that many integrations needs a team working in parallel. A freelancer will either take too long or subcontract parts of it, which means you are paying a freelancer to manage other freelancers. At that point, just hire the agency.

### Ongoing marketing and SEO

If you need ongoing Google Ads management, a content marketing team writing weekly blog posts, social media management, and active SEO campaigns, an agency is built for that. They have specialists for each channel. A freelancer can set up your SEO foundation and advise on strategy, but running a full marketing operation is a team job.

### Brand strategy and design

If you need a full brand identity before the website, an agency with a design team is the better choice. Logo design, brand guidelines, color systems, and typography standards are a different discipline than web development. Some freelancers do both well, but most do not.

### You need account management

Some business owners want a dedicated account manager, regular reporting calls, and a structured project process. If that is important to you, an agency provides it. A freelancer gives you direct access to the person doing the work, but not a formal account management structure.

## When a freelancer is the right choice

### Simple to mid-size websites

If you need a 5 to 15 page website for a local business, a freelancer is the better choice. The scope does not require a team. One person can design, build, and deploy it in a reasonable timeframe. An agency will do the same work but charge you for the overhead of having a team that is not fully utilized on a small project.

### Direct communication

When you hire a freelancer, you talk to the person building your site. Not a project manager who relays messages to a developer who relays questions to a designer. If you want to change the headline on your Home page, you tell the person who is going to change it. That is faster and produces better results because nothing gets lost in translation.

For local businesses especially, this matters. You know your business. The person building your site needs to hear that directly, not through a filter.

### Lower cost

A freelancer charges less than an agency for the same scope because the overhead is lower. No office, no project manager, no sales team, no account management layer. My 5-page Starter site is $447. An agency's equivalent is $3,000 to $5,000. The output is comparable for a site this size. You are paying for the agency's process and infrastructure, not a better website.

### Faster turnaround

A freelancer can start your project this week. An agency typically has a sales process, a discovery phase, a kickoff meeting, and a project schedule that puts your start date weeks out. For a small site, that timeline is unnecessary. If your current site is down or you need something live before a busy season, a freelancer can move faster.

## What about accountability?

The most common concern with freelancers is accountability. If the freelancer disappears, who finishes the project? It is a fair question.

The answer is the same for both: check their work before you hire them. A freelancer with a portfolio, a contract, and references is accountable. An agency with a portfolio, a contract, and references is accountable. The structure is different but the risk is the same. Agencies go out of business too.

The real protection is ownership. If you own your domain, hosting, content, and source code, you can switch to anyone if the relationship breaks down. I cover this in detail in my post on [website ownership](/website-ownership-domain-hosting-content/). If you do not own your assets, it does not matter whether you hired a freelancer or an agency. You are stuck either way.

## Red flags when hiring a freelancer

- **No portfolio or only template demos.** If they cannot show you live websites they built for real clients, do not hire them. A Dribbble profile of mockups is not a portfolio.
- **No contract.** A freelancer who works without a contract is a freelancer who can disappear mid-project with no recourse. A one-page agreement covering scope, timeline, payment, and ownership is the minimum.
- **No deposit.** Freelancers who do not ask for a deposit are either desperate or inexperienced. A 50% deposit is standard. If they do not ask for one, they may not be taking the project seriously.
- **Communication goes dark.** If a freelancer takes 5 days to reply to an email before you have hired them, it will be worse after you pay. Responsiveness during the sales process predicts responsiveness during the project.
- **Will not provide references.** A good freelancer has past clients who will vouch for them. If they say they cannot share client names due to NDAs, ask for a single reference. If they cannot produce one, walk away.

## Red flags when hiring an agency

- **High-pressure sales tactics.** "Sign today or the price goes up" is not how reputable agencies operate. A good agency gives you time to decide.
- **No clear point of contact.** If you cannot identify who will be managing your project before you sign, you will be passed between departments after you do.
- **Vague scope and pricing.** If the proposal does not specify exactly what you get, how many pages, what features, and what the ongoing costs are, you will be upsold later.
- **They own your domain and hosting.** Some agencies register your domain in their name and host on their proprietary platform. This makes it nearly impossible to leave. Always own your own domain and hosting account, regardless of who builds the site.
- **No post-launch support plan.** If the proposal ends at launch with no mention of maintenance, updates, or bug fixes, ask what happens when something breaks.

## How to evaluate either before hiring

Before you sign anything, do these checks:

- **Look at their live work.** Do not look at screenshots or a portfolio PDF. Visit the actual websites they have built. Click around. Test them on your phone. Check the page speed. If their client sites are slow, broken, or ugly on mobile, yours will be too.
- **Contact past clients.** Ask for two references and actually call them. Ask: Did they deliver on time? Was the final price what was quoted? Were they responsive after launch? Would you hire them again?
- **Check their own website.** A freelancer or agency with a slow, broken, or outdated website is telling you what they think is acceptable. If they cannot build a good site for themselves, they will not build one for you.
- **Read the contract carefully.** Make sure it specifies what you own at the end, what happens if the project goes over budget, and what the ongoing costs are. If there is no contract, do not proceed.

## Questions to ask during the hiring process

Whether you are talking to a freelancer or an agency, ask these:

1. **"Can I see 3 live websites you have built for businesses like mine?"** Not screenshots. Live URLs you can visit and test.
2. **"Who will actually be doing the work?"** With an agency, the person pitching you may not be the person building your site. Find out who it is and what their experience is.
3. **"What is not included in this price?"** This is the most important question. Find out what triggers additional costs before you agree to anything.
4. **"What happens if I need changes after launch?"** Some include 30 days of post-launch revisions. Others bill hourly from day one. Know the policy before you need it.
5. **"Do I own my domain, hosting, and all the content when this is done?"** If the answer is anything other than a clear yes, do not hire them.
6. **"How long will this take, and what would cause it to take longer?"** A realistic timeline with stated risks is more honest than a promise of "two weeks."

## How to decide

Ask yourself these questions:

1. **How many pages do I need?** Under 15, a freelancer works. Over 15 with complex features, talk to an agency.
2. **Do I need ongoing marketing?** Yes, lean toward agency. No, a freelancer is fine.
3. **What is my budget?** Under $2,000, freelancer. Over $5,000, agency makes sense.
4. **Do I want to talk to the person building my site?** Yes, freelancer. No, agency.
5. **Do I need a brand identity from scratch?** Yes, agency with design team. No, freelancer.

## The bottom line

Agencies are not overpriced. Freelancers are not unreliable. They serve different needs. The mistake is hiring the wrong one for your scope. A $500 freelancer build for a project that needs a $5,000 agency team will fail. A $5,000 agency build for a project a freelancer could do for $500 is wasted money.

Figure out what you need first. Then hire the person who fits it.
