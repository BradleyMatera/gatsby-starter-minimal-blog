A five-page business website needs a Home page, an About page, a Services page, a Contact page, and one social proof page (Testimonials or Case Studies). Each page has one job. The Home page gets people to other pages. The About page builds trust. The Services page explains what you do. The Contact page makes it easy to reach you. The social proof page closes the deal.

Most small business websites fail because they try to do too much on each page or too little across all of them. A five-page site is not a limitation. It is a structure. When each page has a clear purpose, the site converts better than a 20-page site where nothing is findable.

## Page 1: Home

The Home page is not a welcome mat. It is a routing page. Its job is to answer three questions in under 10 seconds: who you are, what you do, and who you do it for. Then it gets people to the right page.

### What goes on the Home page

- **A headline that says what you do.** Not "Welcome to our website." Something like "Plumbing repairs and installations in Winnebago County."
- **A subheadline that says who you serve.** "Residential and commercial plumbing for Rockford and surrounding areas."
- **A primary call to action.** One button. "Get a quote" or "Call now." Not three buttons competing for attention.
- **Three to five service summaries with links to Services.** Short descriptions, not full paragraphs.
- **One trust signal.** A short testimonial, a badge, or "Serving Illinois since 2012."
- **A footer with your NAP.** Name, address, phone number. This matters for local SEO.

### What to avoid

- A full company history. That goes on About.
- Every service you offer in detail. That goes on Services.
- A carousel slider. They slow down the page and nobody clicks past the first slide.
- Stock photos of handshakes. Use real photos or none.

## Page 2: About

The About page is where trust gets built. People hire people they trust, and the About page is where they decide if they trust you. It is not a resume. It is a story about why you do what you do and who you are.

### What goes on the About page

- **Who you are.** A real photo, not a stock image. Your name and role.
- **Why you started the business.** Two or three sentences. People connect with motivation.
- **Your credentials and experience.** Keep it relevant. "Licensed Illinois plumber, 15 years experience" matters more than your high school.
- **Your service area.** Be specific. "Durand, Rockford, Freeport, and surrounding areas."
- **A personal detail.** Not a full bio, but enough to feel like a real person.

### What to avoid

- Writing in third person if you are a solo business. "Bradley Matera is a web developer" sounds like a press release. "I build websites" sounds like a person.
- Listing every job you have ever had. Keep it to what is relevant.
- A wall of text with no headings. Break it up.

## Page 3: Services

The Services page is the most important page for conversion. It is where someone decides if you do what they need. Be specific. Vague service descriptions lose customers.

### What goes on the Services page

- **Each service as its own section with a heading.** "Drain cleaning," "Water heater installation," "Emergency repairs." Not just "Plumbing services."
- **What is included in each service.** Bullet points. People scan.
- **What it costs, or a range.** If you can list prices, do it. If not, say "Contact for pricing" and explain why (materials vary, scope varies).
- **A call to action after each service.** "Get a quote for drain cleaning" linking to the Contact page.
- **FAQs if you get the same questions repeatedly.** "Do you offer emergency service?" "Do you charge for estimates?"

### What to avoid

- One paragraph that says "We offer a full range of plumbing services." That tells the customer nothing.
- Hiding your most popular service at the bottom. Put it first.
- No call to action. Every service section should have a next step.

If you have more than 5 services, consider splitting Services into separate pages. But for a five-page site, one Services page with clear sections works.

## Page 4: Contact

The Contact page should be the easiest page on your site to use. If someone has to think about how to reach you, you have already lost them.

### What goes on the Contact page

- **A contact form.** Keep it short. Name, email or phone, message. Do not ask for their company size, budget, and project timeline on a contact form for a plumbing business.
- **Your phone number.** Clickable on mobile. This is the most important element for local businesses.
- **Your email address.** Not just a form. Some people want to email.
- **Your physical address with an embedded map.** This helps local SEO and helps customers find you.
- **Your hours.** Be honest. If you do not answer the phone after 5 PM, say so.
- **Your service area listed in text.** Search engines read text, not maps.

### What to avoid

- A form with 10 required fields. Three is enough.
- No phone number. For local businesses, the phone number is the primary conversion.
- A CAPTCHA that is impossible to pass. Use a simple spam filter.

## Page 5: Testimonials or Case Studies

The fifth page is social proof. You have two options: a Testimonials page or a Case Studies page. Pick one based on your business type.

### Testimonials page (for service businesses)

- 5 to 10 real testimonials with customer names and locations
- A mix of specific and general. "Fixed my burst pipe in 30 minutes on a Sunday" is better than "Great service."
- Photos of the customer or the work if you have permission
- A link to your Google reviews if you have them

### Case Studies page (for B2B or project-based businesses)

- 3 to 5 detailed case studies
- The problem, the solution, and the result for each
- Real numbers if you have them. "Reduced load time from 4.2s to 0.8s."
- Photos or screenshots of the work

### What to avoid on either

- Fake testimonials. People can tell.
- One-line reviews with no context. "Awesome job!" does not sell anything.
- No names. "A satisfied customer" is not a testimonial.

## How the pages connect

A five-page site works when the pages link to each other intentionally. The Home page links to Services. The Services page links to Contact. The About page links to Testimonials. The Contact page references the service area from the About page.

Every page should have one primary call to action. On Home, it is "view services." On Services, it is "get a quote." On About, it is "see what clients say." On Testimonials, it is "contact us." On Contact, it is the form itself.

If you build this structure, your five-page site will outperform most 20-page sites. Clarity beats volume.

## What NOT to include on a 5-page site

A five-page site is a discipline. The constraint is what makes it work. Here is what to cut:

- **A blog.** If you are not going to write regularly, an empty blog with three posts from 2023 makes your site look abandoned. Add a blog when you commit to posting monthly.
- **A separate page for every service.** If you offer five services, one Services page with five sections is better than five thin pages. Split them into separate pages only when you have enough distinct content to justify it.
- **A photo gallery on its own page.** Fold photos into the relevant page. A gallery page with no context does not convert.
- **Social media feeds embedded on the site.** They slow down the page and they break when the platform changes its API. Link to your profiles instead.
- **A "Links" or "Resources" page.** Unless you are curating something genuinely useful, this is filler that dilutes your site.
- **Pop-ups and exit-intent overlays.** On a five-page local business site, these annoy more than they convert. Put your call to action in the page content.

## How to expand beyond 5 pages when ready

The right time to add pages is when you have content that deserves its own URL. Here is the order I recommend:

1. **Individual service pages** — when your Services page is getting too long or when a specific service has enough detail (pricing, process, FAQs) to fill a page. This also helps with SEO because each page can target a specific service keyword.
2. **A blog** — when you will commit to posting at least monthly. Blog posts are how you capture long-tail search traffic and answer customer questions before they call.
3. **Location landing pages** — when you serve multiple cities and want to rank in each one. Each page needs unique content, not just a city name swap. I cover this in my [local SEO services](/services/local-seo/).
4. **A case study or project portfolio** — when you have enough completed work to show real results with specifics.
5. **An FAQ page** — when you are answering the same five questions over the phone. Move them to a page and link to it from your contact page.

Add pages one at a time. A site that grows from 5 to 8 pages over a year is healthier than a site that launches with 15 pages of thin content.

## Common mistakes on small websites

The mistakes I see most often on five-page sites:

- **Writing for search engines instead of customers.** Keyword-stuffed headings like "Plumber Rockford IL | Drain Cleaning Rockford" make your site read like a spam email. Write naturally. Search engines are smart enough.
- **No phone number above the fold.** On mobile, if a customer has to scroll to find your number, you lose calls. Put it in the header.
- **Using stock photos that look like stock photos.** A photo of a smiling customer service representative with a headset is a red flag. Use real photos of your work, your team, or your location. If you do not have real photos, use clean design instead.
- **No call to action on the Services page.** People read about your services and then... nothing. Every service section should link to Contact.
- **An About page written in corporate-speak.** "Our mission is to deliver excellence." Nobody reads that. Write like you talk.
- **Forgetting to test on a phone.** More than half of local search traffic is mobile. If your site looks bad on a phone, you are losing customers.

## Examples of effective 5-page small business sites

I will not link to specific client sites here, but I can describe what works. A plumber in Rockford runs a five-page site: Home with a headline and three service links, About with a real photo and service area, Services broken into five clear sections with pricing ranges, Contact with a clickable number and embedded map, and a Testimonials page with eight real reviews pulled from Google. The site ranks in the local pack and gets 20-30 calls a month from organic search. It is not fancy. It is clear.

A salon in Springfield runs a similar structure but swaps Testimonials for a Case Studies page showing before-and-after photos of work. The Contact page lists hours, address, and a booking link. No blog, no gallery, no fluff. The site converts because every page has one job and does it.

The pattern is the same: clear headline, real photos, specific services, easy contact, social proof. That is what a five-page site should do.
